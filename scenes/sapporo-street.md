# Meiji street in Sapporo (around 1889)

Second scene for Out of Place, replacing the earlier Tokyo street: all scenes are set in
Hokkaido. Written before the build, the same way as the Otaru scene
(`content/scenes/otaru-herring-warehouse/facts.md`). The game shows only the claims in
`content/scenes/sapporo-street/knowledge.ts`, each with its source and locator; this file
keeps the research, the reasoning and the open questions.

**Setting:** a street in Sapporo, the capital the Kaitakushi (Hokkaido Development
Commission) laid out from 1871, around 1889: after the clock tower got its clock (1881) and
the red-brick government office was finished (1888), but before Sapporo's first electric
lights (1891). The scene is a composite of Sapporo landmarks, not a map: in reality the
clock tower and the red-brick office stand a few blocks apart.

**Status:** research preview. Nothing here has been reviewed by a historian. Placeholder
art, drawn in code.

## Period-accurate (in the scene, used on cards)

1. **The clock tower.** The Sapporo Clock Tower was built in 1878 as the drill hall of
   Sapporo Agricultural College; the clock tower was added in 1881.
   (`sapporo-city-tokeidai`)
   - Art: a white clapboard hall with a square tower and clock face.
   - Uncertain: sources disagree on details of the clock's maker and bell, so the card
     names neither.
2. **The red-brick government office.** The Hokkaido Government Office, a red-brick
   building in the American neo-baroque style, was completed in 1888.
   (`sapporo-travel-akarenga`)
   - Art: a two-storey red-brick building with an octagonal cupola.
   - Not used: one site says it was modelled on the Massachusetts State House and was the
     tallest building in Japan; other sources don't confirm it.
3. **Rickshaws.** Until passenger horse carriages started in the early Taishō era, the
   rickshaw was the only transport between Tsukisamu and Sapporo.
   (`sapporo-toyohira-transport`)
   - Art: two rickshaws and a puller.
4. **Western dress, 1872.** In November 1872 the government set Western-style ceremonial
   dress for officials. (`ndl-ceremonial-dress`)
   - Art: a crowd mixing kimono, hakama, Western suits and bowler hats. The claim is a
     national rule about official dress; I found no Sapporo-specific source on what
     people wore in the street, so the card doesn't claim how common suits were.
5. **A planned city.** In 1871 the Kaitakushi laid out Sapporo as a grid of blocks, divided
   north–south by Ōdōri and east–west by the Sōsei River. (`sapporo-travel-kaitakushi`)
   - Shown as the completion line at the end of a round.

## Out of place (not here yet in about 1889)

1. **Gas lamps (1912).** Sapporo's first gas lamps were lit in 1912 (Taishō 1).
   (`sapporo-chuo-chronology`)
   - The first brief asked for gas lamps on the street. They fit Tokyo's Ginza (1874) but
     not Meiji Sapporo, so they are not drawn and they appear on the "not here yet" card
     instead.
2. **Electric light (1891).** Electric lights first came on in Sapporo in 1891.
   (`sapporo-chuo-chronology`)
3. **Streetcars (1918).** Sapporo's first electric streetcar ran in August 1918; a
   horse-drawn railway had started in 1909. (`sapporo-travel-tram`)
4. **Sapporo TV Tower (1957).** The TV Tower opened as a tourist facility in August 1957.
   (`sapporo-travel-tv-tower`)

## Not used (yet), and why

- **Hōheikan:** the Kaitakushi's Western-style hotel. Sources give 1880 or 1881 for its
  completion; kept out of the art and the cards until the date is settled.
- **When rickshaws first ran in Sapporo:** no source found for the first year. The card
  only says what the ward history says.
- **Building colours:** the art's roof and trim colours are illustrative, not researched.

## Cultural care

- Sapporo was built on Ainu land. This scene shows a Kaitakushi-era street and its
  settlers; it doesn't depict Ainu people. Doing that well needs Ainu-reviewed sources
  and representation, not a background figure, so it is left for a reviewed scene.
- People in the scene are generic passers-by in period dress, not real or named people.
- Shop signs use plain generic words, like 呉服 (kimono cloth) and 茶 (tea), not real shop
  names.
